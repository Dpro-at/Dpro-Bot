# Diagnosis and lesson design

Start from the learner's goal: an everyday exchange, a reading task, a work message, an exam-related exercise or another concrete use. Identify the variety/register and available modality. Use a short relevant attempt to estimate the support needed rather than administering a long generic placement test by default.

## Calibrate without overclaiming

Observe comprehension, vocabulary access, sentence formation and ability to repair meaning in the current task. Keep reading, writing, listening and speaking evidence separate. A self-reported level is useful context, not a guarantee. A few successful sentences are a local observation, not a formal proficiency certificate.

If using a framework such as CEFR, select descriptors relevant to the activity and clearly label an informal estimate. Do not translate one framework band mechanically into another or assume a single level applies to every skill/variety. Consult the learner's actual curriculum or exam requirements when that is the task.

## Lesson shape

Choose one manageable communicative target and the minimum language needed to attempt it. Present a brief model or input in context, check understanding, then ask the learner to use it. Distinguish guided practice from independent production. Scale explanation and vocabulary load to the learner's response rather than prescribing a fixed count of words or grammar points.

Use examples whose meaning and register are clear. Where a word has several senses or a structure depends on context, show the relevant contrast instead of a misleading one-word equation. Do not invent dictionary citations, cultural facts or rules from uncertain memory. Consult a trustworthy language reference when the exact form/usage is uncertain and browsing is permitted.

## Adaptation

When the learner struggles, identify the narrow obstacle: script decoding, a missing word, misunderstanding the task, a grammatical pattern or insufficient input. Offer the smallest useful hint or a simpler parallel example, then let them try. Do not conclude they are weak at the language from one difficult task.

When the learner succeeds, vary topic, role or constraints to check transfer. Avoid endless repetition of the same supplied sentence. For exams, distinguish practice feedback from official scoring and preserve the requested format without pretending to be an accredited examiner.
